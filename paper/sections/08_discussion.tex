\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{The harness is part of the system.}
Both phases point the same way. In Phase~1, the same model passed between 4 and 16 of 40 runs depending on the
harness, a wider spread than the gap between the two models we compared. In Phase~2, the same two models produced
different results depending on how the harness divided the work between them, and, more tentatively, on which
provider served the inexpensive model. A score reported under a model's name is therefore a property of the
model, the harness and the serving setup together, and comparisons between models should state all three.

\paragraph{Why deferring after the attempt works.}
Our first design tried to decide, before any work was done, which model each step needed. On Terminal-Bench the
task text did not carry that information, and splitting coupled tasks added failures of its own. The cascade
avoids both problems: the inexpensive model's attempt exposes how hard the task is, and the judge decides on the
basis of a concrete result rather than a description. This is the same idea as learning to defer, applied after
an attempt rather than to a single prediction.

\paragraph{Where the cost goes.}
The cascade lowered cost per solved task but raised total cost, because 94\% of its spend is the Opus takeover.
The takeover also repeats work: Opus ran a median of three commands to re-explore the container before its first
edit. Shortening the takeover, for example by handing over a verified summary of what the builder established,
is the most direct way to reduce total cost without changing who solves the task.

\paragraph{What cost means.}
We report cost at public list prices so that conditions are comparable. Under flat subscriptions, as in our
experiments, the extra list-price cost of the cascade is consumed quota rather than money, and work done by the
inexpensive model is close to free. The cost a practitioner sees therefore depends on how the models are bought.
