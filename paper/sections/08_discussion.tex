\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{The harness is part of the system.}
Both phases of this study lead to the same conclusion. In Phase~1 we held the model fixed and changed only the
harness, and the results moved considerably. GLM-5.3 Flash passed between 10\% and 40\% of its runs depending on
the harness, a spread of 30 percentage points, while the gap between the strongest and the weakest model
averaged over all harnesses was 14 points (41\% for GPT-6.1 Sol against 27\% for GLM-5.3 Flash). For GPT-6.1 Sol
the harness changed cost more than pass rate: 18 solves under Codex CLI cost \$17.83, and 17 solves under ZCode
cost \$126.05. In Phase~2 we held both models fixed and changed only how our own harness divided the work between
them, and the cascade solved 11 of 36 evaluation runs against 4 of 35 for the strong model alone. That gap is
not statistically significant at our sample size, but it points the same way as Phase~1. A pass rate reported under a model's name is therefore a
measurement of a model, a harness and a serving setup together. We think comparisons between models are only
meaningful when all three are stated, and that the harness deserves to be treated as a design variable in its
own right rather than as a neutral container.

\paragraph{Deciding after the attempt rather than before it.}
Our first design tried to decide, before any work was done, which model each part of a task required. Two
things worked against it. First, Terminal-Bench tasks are tightly coupled: steps share files, intermediate
values and assumptions, so a split that looks reasonable on paper creates hand-offs in which information is
lost. Second, the task text carried little information about difficulty. Inside the harness, Laya's step scores
clustered around the middle of its scale. Offline, on 42 development runs, routers trained on task-text
features reached an AUC of 0.46 to 0.50 when deciding whether a run should be escalated to the strong model,
which is close to chance. The cascade
avoids both problems. It keeps the task whole, and it postpones the routing decision until the inexpensive model
has produced something concrete that the strong model can inspect. In this respect our method is closer to
learning to defer and to answer-level cascades such as FrugalGPT~\cite{chen2023frugalgpt} than to prompt-level
routers such as RouteLLM~\cite{ong2024routellm}, with the difference that the object being judged is the state of a working environment rather than a
single text answer.

\paragraph{What the inexpensive model contributes.}
In the evaluation runs, no task was solved by GLM-5.3 Flash's build alone; every solved run went through an Opus
takeover. One could read this as the inexpensive model adding nothing, but the comparison with Opus alone is
more consistent with a different reading. Opus working from an empty container solved 4 of 35 runs, while the cascade, in which
Opus continued from GLM's partial build, solved 11 of 36. The value of the first stage appears to lie in the work it leaves behind: files
created, dependencies installed and parts of the problem already explored. The strong model then spends its
budget on the remaining difficulty instead of on setup. This suggests that the useful question for cascades of
agents is not only whether the inexpensive model can finish a task, but how much of the task it can move forward
at low cost.

\paragraph{The judge is the weakest stage.}
The judge decides whether a build is shipped or handed over, and its \emph{ship} decisions were often wrong. On
the development tasks, \emph{ship} was correct in 9 of 23 cases, and on the evaluation tasks in none of 8. The
wrongly shipped builds were near misses that passed between 70\% and 93\% of the hidden tests. The judge reviews
the container and the builder's notes but does not run the task's own checks, which are hidden from the agent.
We tried giving the judge a check script written by the builder (v4), and it did not change the judge's
decisions: with every check passing, the judge still sent 8 of 11 builds to Opus, and 4 of those 8 were then
solved. Checks written by the builder reflect the builder's reading of the task, including its mistakes, so they
cannot replace an independent test. In practice the judge acts less as a filter that saves takeovers and more as
the point where the strong model enters the task.

\paragraph{Where the cost goes.}
The cascade lowered cost per solved task by 51\% but raised total cost by 34\% compared with Opus alone. The
reason is that 94\% of the cascade's spend is the Opus takeover. Part of that spend repeats work already done:
a takeover runs a median of three commands to re-explore the container before its first edit, which accounted
for about \$8.87 of \$28.20 of takeover spend on the development runs. A short hand-off note from the builder
(v3.1) did not reduce this, and keeping the Opus prompt cache intact (v3.5) lowered the cost per call but also
the number of solves. Making the takeover shorter without making it less effective remains the clearest path to
a cascade that is cheaper than the strong model in total, and not only per solved task. We expect this to
require a hand-off that the strong model can verify quickly, rather than a summary it has to take on trust.

\paragraph{The serving setup is part of the system too.}
Our third research question was not planned. It arose when the subscription through which Factory served both
models ended during the evaluation, and we had to continue with GLM-5.3 Flash served by Z.ai. The policy of the harness did not change, yet the system around it did: the new provider generated
tokens more slowly, used a higher default reasoning effort, and left the handling of rate limits and broken
streams to our own code. The results in Section~\ref{sec:p2results} are too small to measure the effect of this
change, so we treat it as the motivation for a controlled study. What it does show is that the same model name
can stand for different systems, and that a result which depends on a commercial service can become hard to
reproduce once that service changes or ends. For this reason we kept the harness code, configurations and run
traces of every condition, and they are available from the authors on request.

\paragraph{What cost means.}
We report cost as logged tokens multiplied by public list prices, so that all conditions are measured on the same
scale. This is not what our experiments cost us. Under the flat subscriptions we used, the extra list-price cost
of the cascade was consumed quota rather than money, and work done by GLM-5.3 Flash under a coding plan was close
to free. The cost that a practitioner experiences therefore depends on how the models are bought. Under metered
API pricing, the cascade trades a higher total bill for more solved tasks; under subscription pricing, where the
inexpensive model is nearly free and the strong model is limited by quota, the same design mainly changes how a
fixed quota is spent. We report both total cost and cost per solved task so that readers can judge the method
under either view.
