\section{Phase 2: A Cascade Harness}
\label{sec:p2method}

Phase~1 treats existing harnesses as fixed. In Phase~2 we build our own harness and ask whether its design can
change results while the models stay the same. The harness runs as an external agent inside the Harbor
evaluation framework: it receives the task instruction, issues shell commands in the task container, and reports
token usage per model call, from which we compute cost at public list prices.

\paragraph{First design: splitting tasks into routed steps.}
Our proposal described a harness that splits a task into steps and routes each step to the cheapest capable
model. A planner turns the instruction into a dependency graph of steps, each with an acceptance check; Laya, a
small typed-decision model, scores the capability each step needs; a fixed table maps the score to a model; a
worker carries out the step; and a verifier runs the step's check and escalates one tier on failure.

Terminal-Bench tasks proved tightly coupled. Steps share hidden state, such as files on disk and values that one
step must discover before another can use them. In one run, two steps ran in parallel although the second needed
a seed that only the first could find; the second step spent 95\% of the run's budget and failed. When planners
did split a task, they tended to produce a straight chain of four steps with no parallel work, so splitting added
hand-offs without saving time, and results passed between steps were truncated to 300 characters. Across these
early versions, the decomposition design solved 3 of 37 runs, against 5 of 22 for Claude Sonnet~5.5 alone and
5 of 10 for Claude Opus~5.5 alone. The router also had little signal to work with: Laya's 91 step decisions
clustered around the middle of its scale (mean 1.16, SD 0.19 on 0--2), and 77\% of steps went to the middle tier.
We therefore kept each task whole and moved the routing decision to a point where the task's difficulty can be
observed.

\paragraph{Final design (v2.8): build, judge, take over.}
The harness keeps the whole task in one container and decides who works on it in three stages:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Build.} GLM-5.3 Flash, the inexpensive model, attempts the full task (up to 50 commands and
  25~minutes).
  \item \textbf{Judge.} Claude Opus~5.5 reviews the result once and returns \emph{ship} or \emph{fix}.
  \textit{Ship} ends the run with GLM's solution.
  \item \textbf{Take over.} On \emph{fix}, or when the review fails or GLM never submits, Opus continues in the
  same container with the problems it found and GLM's notes (up to 60 commands).
\end{enumerate}
The decision to defer to the strong model is made after the inexpensive model's attempt rather than from the
task description, in the spirit of learning-to-defer and model cascades~\cite{chen2023frugalgpt}. The strong
model starts from a partly built solution instead of an empty container.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
Opus~5.5 alone and GLM-5.3 Flash alone run inside the same harness with the same command and time budgets, so
the only difference between conditions is who works and when.
