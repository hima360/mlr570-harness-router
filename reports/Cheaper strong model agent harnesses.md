# Shrink the Opus takeover, not the routing

The published work that best fits our cost profile does not route better. It makes the Opus takeover cheaper per turn, without changing what Opus sees or decides. Total cost is 1.34x Opus alone, so we must cut **25.4% of our spend to reach parity** (1 - 1/1.34, computed here). About **52% of all spend is Opus output** (0.94 x 0.55), and the notes record roughly a third of takeover cost as uncached input. Three levers act on those shares and, in the literature, leave solve rate flat. First, stop breaking the prompt cache inside the takeover. Second, truncate old tool outputs verbatim at a token threshold. Third, set effort per message instead of for the whole takeover. Together they plausibly clear the 25% bar while keeping 11/36 solves. That figure is inferred and has not been measured on our harness. An advisor-style split, where GLM keeps the loop and Opus gives capped advice, attacks Opus output directly. It is a riskier bet because GLM solves only about 6% alone. Learned routers, learned critics and cheap best-of-n with a verifier are the least likely to help. Their gains are small even in their own papers, they fall to near random out of distribution, and our own data already shows no separation.

## Routing papers promise 40-98% but deliver under 20% on agents

The classic cascade results are single-query QA. FrugalGPT claims up to 98% savings ([arXiv 2305.05176](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05176)) and RouteLLM 35-85% ([LMSYS](https://lmsys.org/blog/2024-07-01-routellm/)). Neither transfers to agents. Frontier-agent routing lands at **-8% to -19% cost on 25-35-task test sets**: RSI-Router -18.0% on Terminal-Bench 2.0 (n=25) and -8.1% on SWE-bench Verified (n=32) ([arXiv 2609.34712](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.34712)), and LEASH -19.2% on OSWorld, using hidden states we cannot read through an API ([arXiv 2610.22280](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22280)). Two 2026 results argue against step-level model switching for us. Because 98.6% of agent input is cache hits, **delegating turns to a cheaper model raised cost in half the settings tested** ([TurnCost, arXiv 2610.15093](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.15093)). And swapping models mid-trajectory rewrites 61-94% of later actions, so a router scored by replaying logs is invalid ([Replay Gap, arXiv 2608.08239](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.08239)). Every escalation signal in this literature predicts whether the *cheap* model will fail. None predicts whether the *strong* model will succeed after takeover. That matches our "no separation" result, and the 37% ceiling caps what any task router could recover.

## Cache, truncation and per-message effort are the solve-neutral levers

On mechanism, a cache fix is billing-only. If the model's input is unchanged, solves cannot move. Our cache-kept arm (7/36 vs 11/36, Fisher p = 0.41 per the notes) most likely changed *what Opus saw* by carrying phase-one history. That is a different intervention from a stable, append-only prefix inside the takeover. Cached reads cost about 1/10 of base input, and caching cut agent cost 45-80% across providers ([Don't Break the Cache, arXiv 2601.06007](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06007v1)). Converting our uncached share to cache reads is worth **up to about 30% of total** (inferred). On Opus 5.5, a top-level effort change between requests can invalidate the cache, but per-message effort preserves it ([Claude docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/build-with-claude/effort)).

Verbatim threshold compaction is the best-supported context cut. CliffCompaction drops tool results over 500 characters and keeps the last K turns once context passes a threshold. It **raised Terminal-Bench 2.0 pass rate 2-6 points while cutting cost 40-52%** on Kimi K2.6 and GLM 5.1. On GLM 5.3 Flash it beat Claude Code's own auto-compaction (76.7% vs 71.0%) ([arXiv 2609.26779](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.26779)). LLM summarisation is the wrong variant. It made trajectories about 15% longer and cost a thinking model 9 points ([Complexity Trap, arXiv 2508.21433](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.21433v3)). Once input is mostly cached, trimming text barely helps: compressing terminal output moved Claude Code cost by +1% ([Quesma via braindetox](https://braindetox.kr/en/posts/rtk_terminal_compression_cost_benchmark.html)). So fix the cache first and truncate second.

Effort is the only lever on the 52% output share that keeps Opus doing the work. A per-step effort router cut **reasoning tokens up to 52.7%** with minimal success loss. The same paper found **static low effort degrades agents** ([Ares, arXiv 2603.07915](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07915)), which is exactly what our failed dev run did. The test set was TAU-Bench, BrowseComp-Plus and WebArena, not coding, so the notes' estimate of **7-14% total saving** is unvalidated.

## The advisor split is the big bet, with large downside

Anthropic's advisor pattern inverts our design. The cheap model runs the loop and calls the strong model 2-3 times, usually for 400-700 tokens of advice, capped at `max_tokens: 2048` with about 0% truncation ([advisor docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/advisor-tool)). Published gains are modest, and the comparison is against the cheap model, not the strong one: **+2.7 points at -11.9% cost versus Sonnet alone** ([Anthropic](https://claude.com/blog/the-advisor-strategy)). The Claude-only executor rule means we would rebuild it as an `ask_advisor` tool around GLM. The closest published evidence for that split is Opus 4.8 directing GLM-5.2 on Terminal-Bench 2.0: 69.7 vs GLM alone at 58.4, at 2.9x the cost, with no Opus-alone baseline ([Blackbox](https://www.blackbox.ai/blog/orchestrator-executor-multi-agent-system-for-swe-tasks)). A text-only strong manager over a weak worker matched the strong agent, 62% vs 60% on SWE-bench Lite ([arXiv 2603.26458](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26458)). But a **one-shot strong plan executed by a weak model fell to 29% vs 45%** ([arXiv 2505.20182](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.20182v1)). That is the shape of our failed "GLM fixes from Opus's problem list" run. The split works only when Opus stays in the loop and is re-consulted after GLM acts. DeepSeek Harness offers no usable evidence here. Its published Terminal-Bench figures are single-model, and the strong-planner-plus-Flash-subagents setup appears only in secondary blogs (Unverified).

## Mechanics generalise across benchmarks; learned predictors do not

Cache and truncation results replicate across harnesses (Terminus-2, mini-swe-agent, OpenHands, Claude Code) and benchmarks (Terminal-Bench 2.0 and 2.1, SWE-bench Verified) ([arXiv 2609.26779](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.26779)). They follow from billing arithmetic, not from task features. Learned components break under distribution shift. RouteLLM routers went near random on MMLU ([LMSYS](https://lmsys.org/blog/2024-07-01-routellm/)). SWE-Router lost its edge on a repository-split test set ([arXiv 2607.00053](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.00053)). Benchmark-trained critics scored AUC 0.45-0.48 on production outcomes ([OpenHands](https://openhands.dev/blog/20260305-learning-to-verify-ai-generated-code)). Cheap sampling with a verifier also fails our numbers. Real selectors recover only about half of the oracle gap even at much higher base rates ([CodeMonkeys](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.14723)), while GLM solves 2/34 and our judge's ship calls were right 0/8 times.

## Conclusion

The question shifts from "which model, when" to "how much each Opus turn costs". Our routing ceiling is 37% of Opus spend, and it needs a predictor no paper has. The token-mechanics ceiling covers roughly the whole takeover bill, and it needs no prediction. At n=36 one solve is 2.8 points, and a 4-solve gap is not significant. So prefer changes that leave Opus's input unchanged. Their solve neutrality follows from the mechanism, and five instrumented dev tasks can confirm the cost effect before any held-out spend.
